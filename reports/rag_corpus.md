## The model inputs reproduce the site

The classifier pipeline (median imputer, standard scaler, L1 logistic regression) folds into one linear formula over z-score differences, home minus away:

| Term | Value |
|---|---|
| Intercept (home court, after folding the scaler) | 0.360242 |
| Weight on `regular_net_rating_z` difference | 0.355868 |
| Weight on `regular_win_pct_z` difference | 0.128914 |

Every other column has a weight of exactly 0, and no served profile is missing a non-zero feature, so the imputer never acts and the fold is exact.

Applying the static export's rounding (each home order to 4 decimals, then the neutral average to 4) to 2000 random ordered pairs (seed 20261026):

|---|---|
| Pairs that match the exported `p` exactly | 2000 of 2000 |
| Largest difference | 0.0000 (tolerance 0.0005) |
| Mean difference | 0.000000 |

### `method`

**`method:model-weights`**: How the simulator weighs the two teams (about 161 tokens; keys none)

```text
How the simulator weighs the two teams.
The hist-v2 classifier gives non-zero weight to 2 inputs: net rating and win %, each as a z-score against the team's own league. Every other stat it was offered carries zero weight.
With one team at home, the home team's log-odds of winning are 0.360 (home court) + 0.356 x (difference in net rating z-scores) + 0.129 x (difference in win % z-scores).
The site scores both home orders and averages the two probabilities, so neither team has home court.
One standard deviation of net rating moves the log-odds about 2.8 times as much as one standard deviation of win %, so net rating decides most matchups.
```

**`method:why-teams-are-compared-with-their-own-league`**: Why teams are compared with their own league (about 188 tokens; keys none)

```text
Why teams are compared with their own league.
Raw numbers drift a long way between eras. League offensive rating was about 105 to 108 points per 100 possessions in the late 1980s and 1990s, and about 115 in the 2020s. True shooting rose from about .530 to .580, and three-point attempts went from a small share of shots to about two in five. A model that read raw numbers would judge an older team on a scale that moved, and it would tilt evenly matched cross-era games toward modern teams. So the simulator reads each stat as a z-score: how many standard deviations a team was above or below the average of its own season's league. A team that was dominant for its time scores as dominant, whatever the era. (Source: reports/older_seasons_backtest.md.)
```

**`method:release-limitations`**: Limitations of the hist-v2 simulator (about 272 tokens; keys none)

```text
Limitations of the hist-v2 simulator.
- Cross-era matchups never happened, so these outcomes cannot be validated against real results.
- Results are for a neutral court: each matchup is scored with both teams at home and the two averaged.
- Each team is compared with its own season's league: its stats are read as how far above or below that season's league average it was (a z-score), not as raw numbers, because scoring, shooting and pace changed between eras.
- In practice the model reads two numbers per team: its net rating and its win percentage, each against its own league. Every other stat it was offered carries zero weight.
- Seasons before 1996-97 use ratings, pace and shooting computed from the box score; the NBA's own advanced values exist only from 1996-97.
- Rebound percentages are not used: they cannot be rebuilt from the box score for older seasons.
- No accuracy figure is published for this release.
- The point margin is approximate; typical error on real games was about 11 points.
- Team strength comes from full-season averages, not rosters, injuries, or rest.
```
